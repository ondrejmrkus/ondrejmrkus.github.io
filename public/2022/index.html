2022: Unanswered questions

A yearly reflection on my work and life.
January 15, 2023 · 10 min · Ondrej Markus

2022 was a turbulent year for me:

  • I wrote full-time for 3 months, published 26 articles, but ended up deciding not to pursue writing full-time anymore
  • I grinded for 3 months as a consulting product manager
  • I decided to pivot my focus to game design
  • I moved from Prague to Amsterdam

Last year feels like it had four distinct chapters. So let’s begin by going over each of them to briefly reflect on what happened.

Chapter 1: Full-time writing experiment#

I landed into 2022 on my feet, running fast, with a commitment to publish 2 articles every week for 3 months.

The goal was to test whether I want to pursue writing online to make a living or not.

And I did it. I published 26 articles over 3 months without missing a deadline. Barely. It was fun but very challenging, so I was proud to have made it through with my ego intact.

Then I took 2 weeks to slow down and not do anything but reflect: What do I really want to do next in life?

I came to a conclusion that surprised me: I want to make games. And I definitely want to keep writing, but mostly as a tool for introspection, not as a profession.

Also, another hidden unanswered question of my life popped out: Would I enjoy living in a different country?

I decided to test both of these hypotheses by the end of the year.

Although, I was running out of savings and I knew moving countries will require me to have a solid cash buffer.

(More about this chapter in my Q1 reflection.)

Chapter 2: Grind as a consulting product manager#

Right then, I dusted off my design and product management skills and jumped back to work with Q Designers to lead a 3-month innovation project for a big energy provider in Prague.

My plan was to work 4 days a week on this project and leave the rest for of my time for exploring different ways to make games. That would enable me to save enough money while making progress in what I really wanted to do.

However, after 3 weeks on the project, I realized I’m not spending any time on games at all. The project was renting 100% of my brain, working 6 days a week.

Also, I completely stopped writing, so I investigated in a reflection: Why don’t I do what I say I want?

I was worried I’m actually avoiding work on games because I’m afraid of failing at what I really want to succeed at.

At the same time, I objectively had a lot of project responsibilities on my mind. And even though I had some time to spare, whenever I sat down to work on games, nothing came out. I kept thinking about what needs to be done elsewhere. My attention was completely spent on the job.

I knew that if I want to do the best I can, I have to give it my full attention, so I did. I postponed game design experiments until July.

The product we worked on turned out well, so I’m glad I made this call. Turns out that half-assing several things at once doesn’t work for me.

(More about this chapter in my Q2 reflection.)

Chapter 3: Let’s make games in Amsterdam#

Once I handed over the product, I took a week to just exist in peace. I needed to rest after two and a half months of intense work.

The good news was that I saved quite a lot of money (roughly 7.000 euro) because I had a solid senior-level salary and my living expenses are tiny (by design).

Another important decision happened: We talked about where we want to go with Lin, and she found a six-month course in Amsterdam she wanted to join. Amsterdam was one of our top cities to try living in and the course was starting on 16th September, so we decided on the spot that we are moving to Amsterdam in ten weeks.

For the next two months, I focused on experiments with making board games and video games, going to local game design meetups, connecting with games people online – all this to figure out if and how I actually want to make a living working on games.

In the meantime, I felt like a telemarketer, selling most of the stuff I owned in our apartment in Prague, so we are ready to move to Amsterdam.

The biggest moving challenge was to find a place to live in Amsterdam. Turns out getting an apartment there is next to impossible for a couple of not-employed-at-the-moment people. So we decided to book a room in the Social Hub for the first month or two. We arrived to Amsterdam on 15th September, ready to mingle.

I tried a bunch of stuff in my game design explorations, but looking back, I can see how distracted I was by splitting my attention between game design and preparing to move to a new country on two month’s notice.

I didn’t create anything substantial beside a small card game prototype I play-tested a few times with different groups of people and a couple more untested unfinished prototypes of other board games.

It felt like something was missing to make the game-making explorations feel meaningful, but I didn’t know what.

((More about this chapter in my Q3 reflection.))

Chapter 4: Finding purpose in making games#

The first 3 weeks in Amsterdam felt like a vacation. Partially because the Social Hub is technically a hotel and it looks like one. And partially because everything was new.

I immediately started exploring the city on a bike every day and I joined several Discord servers (mostly board game related) to meet new people, find gaming groups, and make local friends.

Another big question mark on my mind was: Should I find a job? Because the rent was double of what we paid in Prague, so my savings wouldn’t last forever.

Looking for a job was a huge reflection point for me. Two forces were fighting in me over control: the Survivor and the Dreamer. The Survivor yelled at me to get a safe job quickly to stabilize the monthly cash bleed. While the Dreamer whispered in my ear to take a chill pill and have all the time I need to find an opportunity I love instead of jumping on anything just for the feeling of safety which would be quickly followed by the horror feeling of “oh no I hate this job and my life and I can’t get out.”

Both of them had a point. But what is the right balance? I bounced back and forth between whether I should play it safe and pay the price by doing a boring job. Or if I should go only after opportunities I really see myself enjoying because they enable my natural curiosity instead of overriding it with something I don’t care about as much.

I landed closer to the Dreamer’s pitch: be bold (naive?) and do not rush this. Find something truly exciting you’d die for to have a chance to do.

Two things came around during October and November I would put into the die-for-to-do category.

The first one was Synthesis – an enrichment program for kids started by Joshua Dahn – a teacher who previously co-founded the Ad Astra school at SpaceX with Elon Musk.

I was (and still am) blown away by how they use games to create playful but intense learning experiences where kids become world-class problem solvers while having fun playing games together.

My instant reaction was: I want to work there OR I will build something like this on my own.

(Thank you, Michel, for mentioning Synthesis. That was huge for me!)

I immediately applied for an open senior product manager but received a quick “no” as a reply because they were only hiring people close to the Pacific timezone (San Francisco).

(I might also not have been experienced enough for this senior level position, but let’s say it was only definitely because of the time zone issue.)

Disappointed but deeply inspired, I realized what was the missing piece in my game design explorations for the last 6 months that would make the path feel truly meaningful: I don’t just want to make any games. I want to make games to help people learn otherwise hard-to-get skills.

My WHAT got paired with a strong WHY. Now I only needed to figure out HOW exactly am I going to do that?

There are two ways to approach this:

  1. Find someone who already does this and join them
  2. Do it on my own

I decided to try number one first, and pivot to number two if I don’t find something by the end of 2022.

It was the second half of November now and that’s where the second of the two dream opportunities came into play. I found a product manager opening in Lingumi – a game-based tutoring language-learning platform for pre-school kids. I reached out and went through three rounds of interviews over four weeks. But, in the final round, they chose somebody else with more experience with the kind of platform they’re running.

Now it was December 16th, disappointed again but still excited and inspired, I decided to follow the “do it on my own path” while casually keeping an eye on new opportunities.

I came back to Czech Republic for Christmas, recharged my batteries over the holidays, and now I’m ready to jump back into action with a freshly baked plan.

Plans for Q1 2023#

I’m going to split this into two categories:

  1. The big picture: What, Why, How

  2. The detail: Routines and habits designed to help me succeed

The bigger picture#

“Make games that help people learn.”

This is my broad intention for the upcoming months.

WHAT

The first layer of my plan is to imitate what Synthesis is doing with their games – they use them to run learning sessions with kids.

Their games (like Constellations or Art for All) are specifically designed to be played in bigger groups (12+ players) divided into teams of 3 or 4. And there is an adult facilitator (that would be me) who asks questions and gives the kids prompts to help them reflect on the experience so they carry the problem-solving, decision-making, and collaboration skills over into their everyday lives.

So the challenge is two-fold:

  1. Create a playable game prototype (that can be used for this kind of session)
  2. Run the first cohort with a group of 12-16 kids to test the game over multiple sessions

This use-case defines what the game needs to be:

  • Real-time (everyone playing at the same time while talking over Zoom)
  • Multiplayer (there are at least 9 players split into 3 teams)
  • Web-based (so nobody needs to install anything locally but play in their browser)

And there are other constraints and features a game like this needs to have. If you care about this stuff, I’m going to be sharing more about this in my newsletter and on Twitter.

In the meantime, I’m not completely giving up on the option of joining a project where this is already happening, but I no longer want to wait and depend on it.

Searching and interviewing takes a lot of energy I’d prefer to spend on making this happen on my own.

The biggest reason to keep looking for an opportunity to join someone is that I would rather do this with someone than alone. It gets lonely when you work on your own. But I will do this alone if nobody pops out because I would rather work on this alone than on anything else with others – no matter how cool they are.

WHY

I love games as a medium and I care deeply about improving the way people learn.

HOW

I will probably make the game in Unity after talking to a few friends about game engines.

And I will aim to start the first cohort (online over Zoom) before this quarter ends.
